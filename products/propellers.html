Submersible Propellers and Thrusters

Large diameter, slow rotation, long throw — the pump that does not pump, it circulates.

A propeller is specified when the basin has to move as a body of water, not when a pocket of it has to be stirred. The impeller turns at 30 to 130 rpm and pushes a wide, slow jet along the floor — the velocity is high enough to keep solids off the bottom and low enough that the floc survives it.

The distinction matters in a biological train: a high-speed mixer shears the floc and adds turbulence where the process wants a gentle, uniform circulation. Propellers are the right tool for aeration tanks, anaerobic zones and anoxic zones where the hydraulic condition is part of the treatment, not just an accessory to it.

Propeller or mixer?

The same tank, two different jobs.

Both keep solids in suspension. They are not interchangeable, and specifying the wrong one is the most common way a basin ends up either dead or sheared.

Reading the model code. QJB · power (kW) / pole pairs - impeller diameter (mm) / blade count - shaft speed (rpm). Example: QJB5.5/4-2200/3-38 = 5.5 kW, Ø2200 mm, three blades, 38 rpm, 4350 N thrust. To select: give us the basin dimensions, working depth, the circulation velocity the process needs and the solids concentration — thrust, not kW, is what the basin responds to.

Where it is used

Basins that have to move.

Aeration tanks

Keeps the mixed liquor circulating so the diffusers see a uniform load instead of a settling blanket.

Anaerobic and anoxic zones

Circulation without oxygen transfer — the condition denitrification depends on.

Sludge holding tanks

Stops the floor from building a solids bank that later arrives at the dewatering plant in one slug.

Cold-climate basins

Continuous movement at the surface prevents ice formation without a mechanical scraper.
